# Take the Question for a Walk

*Thinking with AI before you know what to ask.*

I went for a walk with a few projects on my mind and an AI in my ear. I wanted to talk through what I was trying to do with each of them, save the conversation, and bring it back to Codex, where an agent could help me make something of it.

There was no especially clever prompt. I had interests, half-formed ideas, things I'd tried, things I couldn't quite put my finger on. Walking gives that sort of material room to move. One thought brings up another; I wander off a little, come back, realize that two apparently separate things belong together. I wanted to see what would happen if I had some company in that process.

At one point, after I'd been explaining an intention for a while, the assistant asked me to put it into a single sentence. A compass for the project. I told it I thought I'd already said it. It could say it for me.

That small exchange gets at something I like about working this way. I'd brought the thought in the form I had it. The assistant could gather it, try saying it back, and give me something to respond to. We didn't have to wait for me to become wonderfully concise before we could get anywhere.

Sometimes the useful prompt is what you have at the end of the conversation.

One of the things I wanted to explore was what people were actually managing to do with AI agents. There's a lot to keep up with, and I wanted help finding the interesting work, understanding it, trying things. I'd already had a few experiences that expanded my sense of what was possible. A fairly ordinary request for help studying some material had turned into an interactive study guide. Wanting a better way to work with my AI art had led to a local gallery where I could browse, mark favorites, and point to particular pieces in later conversations.

I hadn't arrived with a specification for either of those. My thinking doesn't usually arrive shaped like software. I can describe what I'm interested in, what feels awkward, what I'd like to be able to do. An agent can sometimes see a possible tool in that description before I do. Then I can look at it and say: yes, that helps. Or: interesting, but you've missed the thing I care about.

The possibilities made me think of the genie and the business of being careful what you wish for. There's a generous version of that question too: what would be worth wishing for? If more things are becoming possible, what would I actually like to bring into existence?

That's a lovely question to take for a walk. It doesn't need to become a five-year plan before you get home.

As we talked, I started describing an idea I'd had for a workspace. Somewhere to think with an AI, develop a piece of writing, follow an inquiry, make things. Somewhere the work could continue after the conversation ended.

I suspect this is familiar to quite a few people. You read something that stays with you. You have a conversation that opens a door. You see a connection, make an experiment, begin to understand something you couldn't explain before. There can be a lot going on, and yet very little of it becomes something you can hand to another person and say: here, you might find this worthwhile.

I wanted help crossing that distance. An essay, a tool, a small piece of art, perhaps eventually something longer. I wanted to find forms that could carry what was good in the thinking and make it available to someone else. So much of what I've found valuable came to me that way, through someone else's effort to express or make something. I'd like to contribute a little to that exchange.

And there ought to be room for things that don't grow into a grand undertaking. A strange connection. An image. A paragraph with something alive in it. I liked the idea of a public studio alongside more finished work: somewhere an experiment could be shared without first having to apply for project status.

The conversation helped me work out what kind of support would make this possible. Some of that happened through agreement. Some happened when the assistant got close enough for me to hear what was wrong.

I asked for something like an accountability partner. I wanted help keeping an intention alive and actually doing something with it. The assistant reasonably began talking about what we would finish this week, what remained undone, when we'd work on it next.

Ah. I could already hear the nagging.

I'd used the word, so this was useful information. I do want things to happen. But an inquiry often develops by reading around it, following an association, talking until a distinction becomes clear. Deciding in advance that today is Chapter One and tomorrow is Chapter Two can be a rather odd way to help that along.

What I wanted was help remembering why the thing mattered. Where had we got to? What had we found? What was the question that still had some life in it? An intention can be clear one day and strangely difficult to recover the next. I wanted to keep it warm enough to return to.

The assistant called this “continuity of intention,” which felt much closer. And later I added another consideration: meet me where I am now. Yesterday's good idea deserves to be remembered, but it doesn't get to run today's conversation. We might pick it up, change it, or find something else worth attending to.

This was already happening on the walk. I could follow the thought while the agent helped hold the map. Every so often we could look at what had emerged and give it a little structure. That felt like a promising way to work.

There was another exchange I enjoyed, partly because it took two corrections to get where we were going. I wanted help with writing: argument, organization, outlines, the shape of a longer piece. I mentioned that earlier conversations and writing might be useful context. The assistant began expanding this into a rather elaborate account of my intellectual development.

We had acquired quite a lot of machinery for someone who wanted help with an essay. I brought it back to the writing.

It then offered a simpler division: I would bring the thinking; it would provide structure and polish. I had to correct that too. I wanted a thinking partner. It could have ideas as well.

That matters to me. I want questions I wouldn't have thought to ask, connections I might have missed, a challenge when something doesn't hold up. Sometimes it says something that changes how I see the question. I want to be open to that without accepting everything it says just because it has said it beautifully. The conversation gives me something to think with, and the resulting work still has to make sense to me.

By the end, we had a useful description of the partnership: help me think, help me keep the thread, help me shape the work, and help me manage the practical business of making it. I didn't particularly want to operate four different modes. I wanted to get on with it, with some help carrying the parts I tend to lose track of.

After the walk, I brought the transcript into Codex. I'd asked for the full conversation as well as a handoff, because I wanted the next agent to make its own sense of what had happened. The corrections were part of the substance. “Accountability partner” would have been a perfectly tidy summary of the wrong idea.

From there we looked at related approaches, revisited earlier attempts, and built the first version of what I'd started calling Workspace OS. The name is doing a little more work than the machinery at this stage. Mostly it's a small set of files: what we're working on, decisions and why we made them, the material for each piece, and somewhere to pick up again. We keep earlier versions too, so we can change our minds without losing the work.

The agent maintains these as we work. I can talk, ask questions, revise a draft, change my mind. It keeps enough of a record that next time we have a useful place to start. Other projects can stay in their own homes; we can look more closely when one becomes relevant.

I was quite protective of the ordinary Codex collaboration. I already liked it. I didn't want to bury it under instructions about how to be helpful. At the same time, I didn't want simplicity to become a rule against building something good. The study guide and the art gallery had shown me that a little software could open a door I hadn't noticed. The question was whether an addition helped us do something worthwhile, and whether I could understand and use it.

Once we had work to return to, I could take it out for another walk. I asked Codex to prepare a short text file with the relevant context, pasted it into a voice chat, and continued the conversation there. Afterwards, I brought the conversation or a written handoff back. A small, fairly ordinary move, but it meant the thinking could travel with me.

Looking back through the conversations, the useful habit I notice is a willingness to keep saying what I mean. If an answer sounds plausible but doesn't quite touch the point, I can try again. Give an example. Ask what else might be possible. Sometimes I only understand what I wanted after hearing an answer that isn't it.

That's part of the pleasure of conversation. You can arrive uncertain and become clearer together. The assistant can contribute without its first interpretation becoming the shape of the whole project. I can wander without having to remember every turn myself.

This essay came out of that process too. During the first walk, I suggested writing something public about what we were discovering. The assistant suggested making it through the very process we were talking about. Later, a voice conversation helped me see that the opening needed to begin with the walk itself. Back into Codex went the handoff, and the writing changed.

I'm still finding out how well the workspace will support this over time. What I have so far is a way to carry a thought from conversation into work, and then bring the work back into conversation. That's enough to be interesting. There are things here now that I can read, use, question, and make better.

If this sounds inviting, you don't need to begin by building a workspace. Bring something you haven't quite worked out. Tell the agent what draws you to it, what you've tried, where you get stuck. Ask it to think with you and help you notice what is taking shape. Let it do some of the gathering and formulating. You can tell it when it gets you wrong.

Then, when something seems worth keeping, make a little of it. A note, a draft, an experiment. Something you can come back to, or give to someone else.

A walk is a perfectly good place to start. The question can come along unfinished.
