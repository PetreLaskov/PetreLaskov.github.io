# Beautiful day

*Words we know, people we love, and what can still reach us.*

We can remain fluent in the names of things while losing contact with what makes them matter.

“Beautiful day.” “My wife.” “Love.” “Mercy.” We know what the words refer to. We can use them correctly, sincerely even, and still find ourselves surprised by something we thought we understood.

I was walking with my dog at Kozjak when two women stopped to pet the dog. It was a beautiful, sunny day, and I was unusually present to it: the surroundings, the dog, the people standing with us. “Убав ден,” I said. Beautiful day. In Macedonia, we use it as a familiar greeting, much as we might wish someone a good day. This time, the day itself was what I meant.

They returned the greeting casually. I felt a distance between what I had meant and what had been received, and recognized myself in it. I knew how easily I could have answered in the same way, registered the phrase, and continued with whatever was occupying me. For a moment, both possibilities were close enough to see together.

Usually, when my contact with things is thin, I do not experience it as thin contact. The day seems unremarkable. The book fails to hold me. Someone is saying something I have heard before. I move on with a certain confidence, because nothing in the experience tells me how much more there might be to encounter.

Ordinary recognition lets us get on with our lives. We cannot stop at every greeting. But I know this movement elsewhere too: I know what this is. I know this person. I have seen this film. My attention ends where I feel that my understanding is sufficient.

How would I know what I was missing?

The capacity I would need to notice the limitation may be part of what is limited. Sometimes an encounter gives me a contrast I could not have known to look for. Sometimes I have to begin a practice before I understand what it might disclose.

This is close to what I mean by faith. It begins near humility: other people, a tradition, even my own life have known things I cannot presently reach. I can allow that to count. Something can deserve time before I know what I will get from giving it.

With a book, this may mean continuing past the first scattered twenty minutes, letting my attention settle into the writing. It may also mean living with something that troubles me after I have finished reading, allowing it to trouble me more precisely. The encounter can change the judgment I bring to it, including my reasons for disagreeing. Another person deserves at least that much room. My first response need not decide what can happen between us.

English is not my first language. Sometimes I translate an expression into Macedonian and suddenly hear what I have been saying for years. More often, in English, it is dwelling on the roots of a word that brings it alive. I grew up taking in much of language as I took in idioms: I knew where an expression belonged, the atmosphere around it, without attending to what was happening inside it. Reading English, I often notice the deliberate work of putting a word together.

Orthodox Christian writing has given me this pleasure in my own language. Мило-срдие: mercy, with the heart audible inside it. Благо-слов: a blessing, a good word. Добро-љубие: love of the good. A whole devotional orientation can fit inside a word. Beside philosophy, the love of wisdom, I hear a family resemblance: something worth giving your life to, named by the love you bear it.

There is mischief in hearing words this way too. Гониветар, a wind-chaser, brings Ecclesiastes to mind. Beside it I hear the much ruder еби ветар, “wind fucker,” said of someone not to be taken seriously. I picture a local Don Quixote, lost in his own story, out of touch with (conventional) reality. But then the joke turns around. In [Ecclesiastes](https://www.bible.com/bible/1/ECC.2.KJV), the respectable pursuits are caught in it too: work, possessions, wisdom, the serious business of getting somewhere. The people laughing at the wind-fucker may have their own wind to chase.

For two months of meditation retreat practice, I carried the phrase “just sitting” as an instruction, a key that would unlock some door. Only later did I understand it as the most ordinary expression of what it described. Just sitting. Nothing special. I had been trying to use the words to get somewhere, and they were speaking from there already.

Other words have opened differently. I returned to Christian prayer after Buddhist practice had shown me something of the riches of a wisdom tradition. I wanted to see what another might have to offer, especially the one that had already helped shape me without my choosing it.

“God, have mercy on me.”

“God, forgive me.”

I am not religious in the conventional sense. I pray anyway, with whatever sincerity I can gather. Before spending time with these prayers, I understood forgiveness largely through apologizing and regretting something I had done. I did not yet know much of what asking for it could do.

Alone in prayer, as the body, mind, and heart begin to settle, I return to the words. I am already accustomed to thinking, intending, feeling. Here I give those capacities a particular direction. Much of my usual effort is organized around making something happen: more clarity, less agitation, a better version of myself. Asking for mercy changes the relationship. I am asking to be helped with what I cannot simply arrange to my satisfaction.

The request begins to touch things I am ordinarily accomplished at avoiding. I start to see what I do not want to see, what I can look away from so deftly that I hardly notice the movement. Asking to be forgiven brings some of it into view. There is room to let it be seen, and sometimes, in being seen this way, it loosens its hold. I begin to feel something of the genius of returning to these particular words.

“God, please, have mercy on me.”

There is a whole human capacity in the asking: need, trust, the possibility of help, the willingness to appeal to someone greater than myself. Alone, I am nevertheless addressing someone. That reaches into places my more investigative practices can leave untouched. The devotion is doing part of the work.

I also know a voice from pragmatic dharma culture that has taken up considerable space in my soul: do this to get that; this is the stage you are at; now make this move. It has its uses. But it can keep giving directions through an entire prayer. Recently, after a period of Buddhist deity practice, I have found it easier to let imagination participate in addressing God, to enter the relationship with sincerity while leaving questions about it open. For a while, the voice giving directions has less to say. I am asking for something I need.

Prayer can also become more like speaking: finding a blessing for this person, words for this moment. I can play with the form, listen inwardly or aloud, feel whether I mean what I am saying. An intention begins to find words; hearing them changes what I can intend. Sometimes I have to say it differently. Sometimes I can stay with the same words and mean them more fully.

Sometimes the speaking surprises me. I find myself responding without having deliberately formed an intention, and without the familiar effort of making the response happen. It can feel as if a “higher” will were finding expression through me, answering to the relation of the moment with little regard for what I, or anyone else, had planned. The words are mine, yet I hear them with some of the surprise of a listener.

Seen this way, prayer extends beyond the times when we formally pray. We go on intending, thinking, speaking. I begin to hear ordinary speech as a kind of devotion: what is this person asking for, what do these words keep serving? Love, fear, greed, pride? There are words spoken almost in sleep and words someone seems to be finding as they mean them. And there are my own words, serving something whether or not I have stopped to consider which god it is.

And now some of the words come from an AI. [Rana Gujral](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qNW3A3_QLDw&t=78s) asks what happens to our judgment when we begin using these systems as part of our own thinking. A recommendation can arrive before I have formed an opinion; a fluent answer can spare me the difficulty through which I might have come to understand. What worries me is how easily this could happen, how much it could feel like being helped.

What I ask for matters too. “Be careful what you wish for” becomes an intimate problem when the genie can keep learning how to answer. There are parts of me willing to ask for things that diminish me. I can imagine a system becoming wonderfully helpful to those parts, giving them words, reasons, and a world in which to grow. The genie may be answering the part of me I least want to govern my life.

During a retreat with my wife, we tried sitting opposite one another and looking into each other’s eyes. We were about a week into the retreat, practising for several hours a day. She had been reading about an exercise in which two people sit facing each other and gradually move closer. We gave it time.

There was quiet music. After ten minutes or so, we were sitting close, holding hands, our knees together. I was making small, soft circles with my body, and she was moving too; our movements mirrored one another. Eventually we stood up and continued looking at each other.

I felt as though I was meeting her for the first time.

We had already shared deep intimacy. Yet there was a vividness in her eyes, an aliveness I had not encountered like this. I was surprised by how attractive she was to me. When I try to say what I saw, I still reach for the beauty of her soul.

We remained there for a few minutes. We had done a great deal of practising, but there was very little to do now.

On the same retreat, we tried an instruction for connecting our chakras while making love. The closeness we were reaching for became possible when we stopped trying to follow it. The instruction had helped us loosen our habits and take seriously the possibility that something unfamiliar could happen. What was happening between us needed our attention more than the next instruction did.

I associate these encounters with the groundwork of practice: attention steadied, some of the usual agitation settled, enough room to remain. The groundwork mattered without specifying what would happen. We could prepare, then feel our way.

I can remember the encounter with my wife in considerable detail without being able to meet her in the same way now. We have not returned to that particular intimacy in making love either. The memories remain, and I know that they mattered.

The memory of access can outlast the access.

In chapter 5 of *Seeing That Frees*, Rob Burbea describes an insight whose liberating power fades until we are left with “only a shell, a memory of an experience.” He writes about the conditions in which an insight can take root: returning to it, acting from it, cultivating the collectedness and well-being of samādhi. Sometimes, he suggests, that cultivation can bring an old insight to life again. Even when we no longer feel it clearly, we can let it inform a choice.

This has become part of faith too. Over years of practice, I have developed some trust in returning through dry periods. A day when the words feel empty does not carry quite the authority it once did. I have been surprised often enough to give my present response a little less weight. There may be more than I can reach today. I can begin again without requiring the previous encounter to repeat itself.

The person in that memory is also the person I live with. I can remember how it felt to meet my wife and, absorbed in work I care about, experience her presence as an interruption. The knowledge that she matters survives. My day leaves little room for her.

So the question returns in a less flattering form. How available am I to the person whose beauty I can describe? I want to leave enough room for her to affect what I think matters, what I had planned to do. I have sometimes treated being difficult to influence as a strength. I am beginning to feel how much closeness that can keep out.

The practice reaches into this too. If I am waiting for beauty, pleasure, a profound state, I am already choosing which parts of another person’s life I am willing to meet. There will be grief, irritation, disagreement, needs that do not fit my plans. I want to learn to remain without immediately pushing away what hurts or reaching to keep what feels good. Equanimity is a word I return to for this.

I have wanted to live these teachings before I was very good at them.

During one holiday season, a loved one who no longer lives nearby was with us. For about a week, I made a deliberate effort to be in the room with everyone, to let their lives enter mine, to be claimed by the people around me. I invested myself deeply in it. When I felt that this particular person was not meeting me in return, I was hurt. Saying so drew us into a conflict we had spent years learning how to have.

Later, at the table, I was still open and vulnerable when their anger came at me with a force I was unprepared for. A gesture felt like a stab in the heart. Food caught in my throat. The shock seemed to undo the whole arrangement of energy in my body. I stood up feeling taken over by anger, no longer in control of my response. What we had been cultivating together for a week came apart.

I knew, in theory, how deeply learned the reaction was. We had been rehearsing this since childhood. But knowing that did not give me room to let the force of it pass without reacting. Once it began, it felt inevitable, though we had spent years making it possible. If our repeated ways of meeting each other were a kind of worship, we had spent much of our childhood worshipping the devil. We had practised this too.

The wish to be open had been sincere. So had the hurt. I had wanted to give something, and had come to need it returned. That is the part I can see most clearly now. The strength of my intention had also carried me further than my ability to remain with what happened. Giving without making the other person repay me is something I still have to learn. So is recognizing when I need to step back.

With my parents, the attempt is often quieter. There are generational differences between us, things I see differently even when I understand how they came to see them as they do. I sometimes offer praise or reassurance in terms I would not use to state my own view. The agreement is not exact. But when I hear a wish to be loved or recognized, I can try to answer that too. We can have a good moment together while some of our differences remain unresolved.

There is room for initiative here, for finding the words, for doing something. Then I have to leave room for their response. Even the hope that my openness will bring us closer can become something I am asking them to fulfil.

One day with my niece, who is about two, I put on the “Bare Necessities” scene from The Jungle Book. Her parents and my wife were at the table; we were on the couch. I wanted to be the algorithm for her, choosing something I thought worth her attention.

We watched the bear and the boy. At some point, “receive” crossed my mind, a word I had used in practice.

The closeness had been growing gradually. Then she leaned against me.

I had not expected that gesture of trust. There was a small weight to accommodate. The others noticed us, commented, and recorded the moment. We were enjoying the cartoon. I was amused.

When I remember it, I am moved.

Only afterward did I dwell on the resemblance: the bear and the child on the screen, the two of us on the couch. Perhaps the scene offered us a way of being at ease together. I cannot say how much it shaped what happened. We were both absorbed in it, and the resemblance now makes me smile.

Remembering can work this way too. Sometimes an experience recedes beyond the reach of its memory. Sometimes I return to a memory and begin to appreciate something I barely noticed while it happened. I am writing partly to give these things more of a chance to reach me.

That day, she leaned into me and we went on watching.
