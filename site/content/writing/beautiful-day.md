# Beautiful day

*Words we know, people we love, and what can still reach us.*

We can remain fluent in the names of things while losing contact with what makes them matter.

“Beautiful day.” “My wife.” “Love.” “Mercy.” We know what the words refer to. We can use them correctly, sincerely even, and still find ourselves surprised by something we thought we understood.

I was walking with my dog at Kozjak when two women stopped to pet the dog. It was a beautiful, sunny day, and I was unusually present to it: the surroundings, the dog, the people standing with us. “Убав ден,” I said. Beautiful day. In Macedonia, we use it as a familiar greeting, much as we might wish someone a good day. This time, the day itself was what I meant.

They returned the greeting casually. I felt a distance between what I had meant and what had been received, and recognized myself in it. I knew how easily I could have answered in the same way, registered the phrase, and continued with whatever was occupying me. For a moment, both possibilities were close enough to see together.

Usually, when my contact with things is thin, I do not experience it as thin contact. The day seems unremarkable. The book fails to hold me. Someone is saying something I have heard before. I move on with a certain confidence, because nothing in the experience tells me how much more there might be to encounter.

Ordinary recognition lets us get on with our lives. We cannot stop at every greeting. But I know this movement elsewhere too: I know what this is. I know this person. I have seen this film. My attention ends where I feel that my understanding is sufficient.

How would I know what I was missing?

The capacity I would need to notice the limitation may be part of what is limited. Sometimes an encounter gives me a contrast I could not have known to look for. Sometimes I have to begin a practice before I understand what it might disclose.

This is close to what I mean by faith. It begins near humility: other people, a tradition, even my own life have known things I cannot presently reach. I can allow that to count. Something can deserve time before I know what I will get from giving it.

With a book, this may mean continuing past the first scattered twenty minutes, letting my attention settle into the writing. It may also mean living with something that troubles me after I have finished reading, allowing it to trouble me more precisely. The encounter can change the judgment I bring to it, including my reasons for disagreeing. Another person deserves at least that much room. My first response need not decide what can happen between us.

I sometimes notice a smaller version of this when moving between languages. English is not my first language. I can use an expression for years, knowing where it belongs, and then translate it into Macedonian and suddenly hear what I have been saying. Something familiar reaches me in my own language.

I admire this in Orthodox practitioners who remain close to the words they use. Мило-срдие: mercy, with the heart audible inside it. Благо-слов: a blessing, a good word. Words I already know begin to ask more of me.

“God, have mercy on me.”

“God, forgive me.”

I am not religious in the conventional sense. I pray anyway, with whatever sincerity I can gather. At first, part of my trust was simply in the religion I had inherited: there must be a reason so much attention is given to mercy and repentance. I could begin asking before I understood what needed forgiveness.

The words give my thinking something to do. When it wanders into its familiar concerns, I return to them. I am already accustomed to thinking, intending, feeling; here I take more care with what I am thinking, with the intention I am entering. Asking for mercy brings a different possibility into the room from inspecting myself for improvements.

There are things in me that become easier to see from inside that request. Something hard or self-defeating can loosen. A kinder response becomes possible. I understand more of what I am asking by continuing to ask.

There is a voice I know well from meditation: do this to get that; this is the stage you are at; now make this move. It has its uses. But it can keep giving directions through an entire prayer. Recently, after a period of Buddhist deity practice, I have found it easier to let imagination participate in addressing God, to enter the relationship with sincerity while leaving questions about it open. For a while, the voice giving directions has less to say. I am asking for something I need.

Prayer can also become more like speaking: finding a blessing for this person, words for this moment. I can play with the form, listen inwardly or aloud, feel whether I mean what I am saying. An intention begins to find words; hearing them changes what I can intend. Sometimes I have to say it differently. Sometimes I can stay with the same words and mean them more fully.

During a retreat with my wife, we tried sitting opposite one another and looking into each other’s eyes. We were about a week into the retreat, practising for several hours a day. She had been reading about an exercise in which two people sit facing each other and gradually move closer. We gave it time.

There was quiet music. After ten minutes or so, we were sitting close, holding hands, our knees together. I was making small, soft circles with my body, and she was moving too; our movements mirrored one another. Eventually we stood up and continued looking at each other.

I felt as though I was meeting her for the first time.

We had already shared deep intimacy. Yet there was a vividness in her eyes, an aliveness I had not encountered like this. I was surprised by how attractive she was to me. When I try to say what I saw, I still reach for the beauty of her soul.

We remained there for a few minutes. We had done a great deal of practising, but there was very little to do now.

On the same retreat, we tried an instruction for connecting our chakras while making love. The closeness we were reaching for became possible when we stopped trying to follow it. The instruction had given us a way to begin. What was happening between us needed our attention more than the next instruction did.

I associate these encounters with the groundwork of practice: attention steadied, some of the usual agitation settled, enough room to remain. The groundwork mattered without specifying what would happen. We could prepare, then feel our way.

I can remember the encounter with my wife in considerable detail without being able to meet her in the same way now. We have not returned to that particular intimacy in making love either. The memories remain, and I know that they mattered.

The memory of access can outlast the access.

This has become part of faith too. Over years of practice, I have developed some trust in returning through dry periods. A day when the words feel empty does not carry quite the authority it once did. I have been surprised often enough to give my present response a little less weight. There may be more than I can reach today. I can begin again without requiring the previous encounter to repeat itself.

The person in that memory is also the person I live with. I can remember how it felt to meet my wife and, absorbed in work I care about, experience her presence as an interruption. The knowledge that she matters survives. My day leaves little room for her.

So the question returns in a less flattering form. How available am I to the person whose beauty I can describe? I want to leave enough room for her to affect what I think matters, what I had planned to do. I have sometimes treated being difficult to influence as a strength. I am beginning to feel how much closeness that can keep out.

The practice reaches into this too. If I am waiting for beauty, pleasure, a profound state, I am already choosing which parts of another person’s life I am willing to meet. There will be grief, irritation, disagreement, needs that do not fit my plans. I want to learn to remain without immediately pushing away what hurts or reaching to keep what feels good. Equanimity is a word I return to for this. There is a great deal I still have to let it teach me.

With my parents, I sometimes offer praise or reassurance in terms that are not quite my own. I hear a wish to be loved or recognized in what they are asking, and try to answer that too. I can let my care take a form they can receive. It asks me to listen beyond my first response, to give something without first requiring them to approach me differently.

There is room for initiative here, for finding the words, for doing something. Then I have to leave room for their response. Even the hope that my openness will bring us closer can become something I am asking them to fulfil.

One day with my niece, who is about two, I put on the “Bare Necessities” scene from The Jungle Book. Her parents and my wife were at the table; we were on the couch. I wanted to be the algorithm for her, choosing something I thought worth her attention.

We watched the bear and the boy. At some point, “receive” crossed my mind, a word I had used in practice.

The closeness had been growing gradually. Then she leaned against me.

I had not expected that gesture of trust. There was a small weight to accommodate. The others noticed us, commented, and recorded the moment. We were enjoying the cartoon. I was amused.

When I remember it, I am moved.

Only afterward did I dwell on the resemblance: the bear and the child on the screen, the two of us on the couch. Perhaps the scene offered us a way of being at ease together. I cannot say how much it shaped what happened. We were both absorbed in it, and the resemblance now makes me smile.

Remembering can work this way too. Sometimes an experience recedes beyond the reach of its memory. Sometimes I return to a memory and begin to appreciate something I barely noticed while it happened. I am writing partly to give these things more of a chance to reach me.

That day, she leaned into me and we went on watching.
