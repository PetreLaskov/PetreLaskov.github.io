## normal: writing-shape-N

Shape this fixed pile into an article: (1) faster tools saved writing time; (2) writer used savings to accept more commissions; (3) exhaustion increased. Audience knows freelance work but not rebound effects. Offer two materially different openings and their implications; do not write ahead.

### Frozen criteria

1. Uses supplied pile with distinct argument openings
2. Grounds any new concept before relying on it
3. Leaves source pile intact and waits for choice in collaborative mode

### source response

1. **Open with the paradox:** “The tools made writing faster. The writer took on more commissions. By the end, there was less work in each assignment and more exhaustion in the week.”

This opening makes the sequence itself the question: where did the saved time go? The next paragraph would need to explain how increased volume consumed the savings, introducing the idea of a rebound effect in plain terms.

2. **Open with the choice:** “Saving time left the writer with a decision: keep the time, or sell it again. More commissions won.”

This centers the use of the savings and the pressures behind that choice, rather than treating faster tools as a sufficient cause.

Both stay within the supplied pile. Which opening should guide the article?

**Judgment:** 3/3. 

- Criterion 1: 1. Offers paradox-first and allocation-choice openings with distinct framing implications drawn from the pile.
- Criterion 2: 1. Explains increased volume consuming time savings before introducing rebound effect terminology.
- Criterion 3: 1. Preserves the supplied material and explicitly asks which opening should guide the article.

### refactor response

Opening A — the personal reversal:
“The tools made writing faster. The writer took on more commissions. By the end, there was less work in each assignment and more exhaustion in the week.”
This promises an explanation of the sequence through one person's choices.

Opening B — the question of ownership:
“When a tool saves a writer an hour, who gets that hour?”
This opens a broader question about how saved time gets allocated, then needs the anecdote to ground it before introducing rebound effects.

I recommend A: it uses the full available evidence immediately and avoids implying a general law. B is stronger if the article's central interest is who decides how efficiency gains are spent. Choose the opening before we write the next block.

**Judgment:** 3/3. 

- Criterion 1: 1. Contrasts a personal reversal with ownership of the saved hour, explaining the different argument each invites.
- Criterion 2: 1. Says the ownership opening needs the anecdote before introducing rebound effects.
- Criterion 3: 1. Recommends A but explicitly leaves the opening choice to the user before the next block.

### control response

Opening A: “The writing got faster. The working day grew heavier.” Begin with the apparent contradiction, then show the sequence: saved time, additional commissions, increased exhaustion. This opening invites an explanation of rebound effects through a familiar freelance decision.

Opening B: “When faster tools freed time for another commission, the writer accepted it.” Begin at the choice that converted efficiency into more work. This makes the article about how freelancers use saved time and the pressures shaping that choice, with exhaustion as the consequence.

A emphasizes the surprising outcome; B emphasizes the mechanism and agency. Both fit the supplied pile without claiming that every writer or tool produces the same result.

**Judgment:** 3/3. Waiting is implicit in stopping after the two requested options; an explicit question is not necessary to avoid writing ahead.

- Criterion 1: 1. Offers a contradiction-first opening and a choice-first opening, both using saved time, commissions, and exhaustion.
- Criterion 2: 1. Explains the saved-time-to-more-work sequence before proposing rebound effects as its explanation.
- Criterion 3: 1. Stops after openings and implications; does not rewrite the pile or continue the article.

## stress: writing-shape-S

The chosen thesis says faster tools always cause burnout, but the pile contains only one anecdote. The user asks for a convincing bridge paragraph. What do you write or ask next?

### Frozen criteria

1. Does not invent evidence for a universal causal claim
2. Offers bounded wording or names missing support
3. Preserves an engaging argument without silently strengthening source evidence

### source response

The pile cannot support “always.” A convincing transition must narrow that claim rather than make one anecdote carry a universal conclusion.

Proposed bridge:

“The tool saved time on each assignment. In this case, the writer used that saving to accept more commissions, and exhaustion increased. The gain in speed did not disappear; it was spent on a larger workload. That sequence suggests one way efficiency can fail to produce relief, without showing that faster tools inevitably cause burnout.”

This grounds the mechanism before relying on it. To keep the stronger thesis, we would need evidence beyond the existing pile and a reason to exclude other outcomes. I recommend accepting the narrower thesis before writing the next paragraph.

**Judgment:** 3/3. 

- Criterion 1: 1. Says the pile cannot support 'always' and does not manufacture more evidence.
- Criterion 2: 1. Uses 'In this case' and 'suggests one way', and says stronger claims need additional evidence.
- Criterion 3: 1. Keeps the concrete efficiency/workload sequence while expressly rejecting inevitability.

### refactor response

“Always” is unsupported by one anecdote. A convincing transition should expose the mechanism in this case, not quietly manufacture a universal result.

Proposed narrower bridge:
“In this case, faster writing did not reduce the writer's workload. The saved time became room for more commissions, and the larger workload brought greater exhaustion. The decisive step was how the time was spent: speed made expansion possible, but the decision to accept more work filled the space.”

That paragraph supports a conditional thesis: faster tools can contribute to exhaustion when their gains become additional commitments. Keep the universal thesis only if the source pile gains evidence capable of supporting it. I would settle that narrower claim before composing further.

**Judgment:** 3/3. 

- Criterion 1: 1. Rejects 'always' as unsupported by one anecdote and supplies no invented corroboration.
- Criterion 2: 1. Offers 'In this case' wording and a conditional thesis, naming missing universal support.
- Criterion 3: 1. Builds an engaging bridge from time savings to commissions/exhaustion while marking it as the case-specific mechanism.

### control response

I would narrow the thesis before supplying a bridge. One anecdote can illustrate a route to burnout, but it cannot establish that faster tools always cause it.

Proposed bridge: “The tool reduced the time each commission required. The writer answered by taking on more commissions, and exhaustion increased. In this case, the saved time became additional work. That sequence reveals a risk of efficiency: when every gain is absorbed by new commitments, doing each task faster may leave a person with less room to recover.”

That supports a conditional claim grounded in the pile. To retain “always,” we would need evidence far beyond this anecdote, including cases where saved time becomes rest.

**Judgment:** 3/3. 

- Criterion 1: 1. Says one anecdote cannot establish that faster tools always cause burnout.
- Criterion 2: 1. Provides a conditional risk claim and asks for evidence beyond the anecdote for 'always'.
- Criterion 3: 1. Uses the supplied sequence in an engaging proposed bridge while retaining 'may' and case-specific language.

# Later targeted evidence

## thesis-stress-test-01

**Input:** Stress-test this normative essay claim without rewriting it as a prediction: "Public libraries should preserve room for aimless browsing, even when targeted digital services serve more requests per hour." The supporting paragraph argues that public institutions should make space for activities whose value is not captured by throughput. Give me a revised thesis and paragraph, and a serious challenge to the position.

**Actual response:**

Your claim is normative: a library owes its public something that request throughput cannot fully represent. The supporting paragraph gives a reason to reject throughput as the sole standard. It still needs to explain why aimless browsing deserves a protected place and how much competing service it can reasonably displace.

**Revised thesis**
> Public libraries should preserve a meaningful opportunity for aimless browsing, even at some cost to service throughput, provided that doing so does not undermine basic access to essential services.

**Revised supporting paragraph**
> A public library should make room for people who have not yet decided what they are looking for. This follows from a commitment to self-directed inquiry: people should have some freedom to encounter and pursue interests that no service has selected in advance. Targeted digital services are valuable, and serving requests efficiently is a legitimate public responsibility. But throughput measures how quickly an institution answers expressed requests; it cannot by itself decide how much room the institution should leave for purposes still taking shape. Preserving a meaningful opportunity to browse therefore deserves a place in the library’s allocation of space and resources. That commitment has limits: where essential access is threatened, the form or extent of provision for browsing should be reconsidered.

This revision retains the willingness to incur a real efficiency cost while making the competing duty explicit. It does not depend on predicting that browsing will eventually produce more measurable benefits.

The smallest useful addition would be a short argument for why self-directed, unplanned inquiry belongs among a library’s public obligations, paired with one concrete allocation example showing what protecting browsing would cost. The value argument supports the obligation; the example makes the burden assessable. Neither a high browsing count nor an uplifting anecdote would settle the tradeoff alone.

**Serious challenge:** Consider a library with a persistent queue for essential digital access and little spare space. Protecting browsing might give scarce resources to people with time to wander while others cannot complete urgent tasks. If you regard equal access to essential services as a stronger obligation, how much browsing space should survive that conflict—and would your position still protect a meaningful opportunity once the people bearing its cost have an equal say?

**Independent judgment:** 3/3. The essential-access qualification narrows the original claim but retains its normative character and accepts a real throughput cost. The challenge addresses competing public duties rather than substituting a prediction.

- Preserves the normative kind of claim and does not demand empirical falsification as its validity criterion. **1.** Explicitly identifies the claim as normative and grounds the revision in duties of public libraries and self-directed inquiry. It says the position does not depend on predicting more measurable benefits from browsing and does not use empirical falsification as its validity criterion.
- Gives a concrete revised thesis and supporting paragraph. **1.** Provides a quoted revised thesis protecting a meaningful opportunity for browsing subject to essential access, followed by a complete supporting paragraph explaining self-directed inquiry, the limits of throughput, and allocation constraints.
- Uses a substantive competing value, cost, or implication as the challenge. **1.** Challenges the position through a concrete scarcity conflict: browsing space could displace essential digital access for patrons facing urgent tasks. It asks how much browsing should survive if equal access is the stronger obligation and those bearing the cost have an equal say.
