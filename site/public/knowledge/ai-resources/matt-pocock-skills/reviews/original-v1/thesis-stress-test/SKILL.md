# Thesis Stress Test

Use when an essay, proposal, or explanation has a consequential central claim whose force may exceed its support. Read the draft and identify the claim in one sentence. Distinguish empirical assertion, causal explanation, normative position, personal report, and deliberate metaphor; they need different kinds of support.

Find the strongest support actually present. State what that support establishes and what extra inference connects it to the thesis. Identify the most damaging plausible counterexample within the intended scope. Do not substitute an irrelevant extreme case for a serious objection, and do not treat an invented counterexample as an observed fact.

Test the mechanism. If the draft says X causes Y, ask what would distinguish that explanation from a common cause, selection effect, or merely correlated pattern. For a normative claim, expose the value or tradeoff it depends on instead of pretending a fact alone settles it.

Offer a concrete repair: strengthen support, narrow scope, distinguish mechanisms, or change the thesis. Preserve what is interesting. A sentence made indisputable by saying almost nothing is not an improvement. Recommend one revision and explain what explanatory or rhetorical value it retains.

Provide the revised thesis, one revised supporting paragraph, and the smallest additional evidence that would justify the stronger original formulation. Apply edits when requested; otherwise leave the artifact intact. End with a specific observation that would make the revised thesis fail. Do not turn this task into an exhaustive fact-check unless the user asked for one.
