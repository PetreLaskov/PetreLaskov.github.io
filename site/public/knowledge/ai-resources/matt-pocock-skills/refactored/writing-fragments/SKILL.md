# Writing Fragments

Explore material for writing without deciding its outline. Capture what might survive into the eventual piece: sentences, scenes, claims, questions, code, dialogue, metaphors, and useful half-thoughts.

Read the user's initial material for fragments immediately. Use the supplied destination; if missing, ask once for it. Keep one Markdown file with a working-title H1 and fragments separated by horizontal rules. Preserve the natural size and form of each fragment. Do not add tags, a contents list, body headings, or an outline.

Interview responsively. Choose a question that might reveal a concrete instance, a surprising distinction, an objection, or language worth keeping. Follow a productive answer before changing topics. Offer wording as a proposal, and seek a leading word when a recurring idea actually needs a name; do not force one.

Append useful fragments without a permission exchange for each save. Capture material from either participant, but label invented scenes, uncertain quotations, and proposed authorial claims in plain prose where their origin would otherwise be misleading. Never turn an assistant's invented experience into the author's memory.

Reread the file before every write. Append to its current contents; preserve user edits, deletions, and ordering. On a requested rewrite, cut, or merge, change only the identified material.

Keep the exploration open. When answers become repetitive, offer a pause or point to a promising unresolved seam. Move to outlining or composition only when the user chooses that transition. End with the saved path and a brief note about the strongest live material, not a surprise article.
