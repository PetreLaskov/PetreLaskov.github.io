# The Six Good-Place Traps

Most practice does not stall by failing. It stalls in a good place - somewhere genuinely useful that quietly becomes a substitute for going any further. Shinzen names six of these. Each one is real progress. Each one can also become the spot where you stop.

**The map trap.** So much time and energy goes into finding the right model, teacher, system, or set of conditions that little is left for actual practice. Maps are tools, not the territory and not the work. The test is plain: is the map serving your concentration, clarity, and equanimity, or replacing the sit?

**The fundamentalism trap.** A belief structure produces real happiness and real improvement in behavior - and then closes the horizon, so no further question, learning, or change can get in. Keep the gratitude for what it gave. Check whether inquiry has quietly shut down behind it.

**The tranquility trap.** Calm concentration becomes pleasant and refreshing, year after year, and stops developing. The antidote is to bring sensory clarity inside the rest: distinguish the actual flavors of restfulness instead of only enjoying the calm. (See [Focus on Rest](focus-on-rest.md).)

**The realms-of-power trap.** Archetypes, special powers, out-of-body experiences, past-life material, visions - they feel like movement toward something deep, and they are usually a move sideways. The antidote is unglamorous: treat the content as ordinary sensory events and meet it with concentration, clarity, and equanimity like anything else. Strangeness is not depth.

**The enlightenment trap.** Genuine, even permanent, no-self realization can make a person believe they know more than they do, and start teaching from there too soon. Real realization does not cancel the parts of a person that are still unfinished. The antidote is courageous feedback from senior teachers, from peers, and from the environment. As Shinzen puts it, "today's enlightenment is tomorrow's mistake."

**The observer trap.** This one is specific to mindfulness. Subtle image, talk, and body sensation quietly assemble a sense of someone meditating - someone standing back here, watching and directing the process. The fix is not a stronger watcher. It is practice becoming so habitual that concentration, clarity, and equanimity eventually enfold and dissolve the very arisings that were building the meditator, until, in Shinzen's phrase, the mindfulness "meditates you."

None of the six is a sign you went wrong. Each is a sign you arrived somewhere worth not mistaking for the destination.

## When A Good Place Has Become A Trap

| You notice | The move back to practice |
| --- | --- |
| most of your effort goes to maps, teachers, or ideal conditions | return to actual practice time; test whether the map serves CCE |
| a belief has made further questions feel unnecessary | keep the gain, reopen the inquiry |
| calm is stable but nothing develops | clarify the flavors of rest instead of resting in the mood |
| unusual powers or phenomena feel like the point | deconstruct them as ordinary sensory events |
| realization is quietly raising your authority | seek senior, peer, and environmental feedback |
| a subtle "meditator" is watching from back here | keep practicing until CCE includes the watcher-making sensations |
